Introduction

The Financial Reality of Higher Education

College is often described as an investment in the future, but how strong is the return after graduation?

Many students pursue higher education to secure stable careers or deepen knowledge in areas they care about. But in the U.S., continuing education has become exceptionally expensive due to rising tuition costs. Many students rely on loans that must be repaid once graduates begin earning, often leaving them with significant student debt.

Using national institutional data, this interactive article explores how tuition costs, student debt, and post-enrollment earnings vary across states and types of colleges, offering a clearer perspective on the financial realities of higher education.

What to Look For

A gap in debt does not automatically mean a similar gap in earnings. Comparing both measures side by side helps show whether higher borrowing appears to correspond to stronger outcomes later on.

Visualization 3

Is there a relationship between median student debt and post-enrollment earnings?

If higher debt is “worth it,” we might expect institutions with more borrowing to also show higher earnings later. This scatterplot compares each institution’s median student debt to median earnings 10 years after entry.

Hover points for sample values.

Interpreting the Pattern

Look for the overall direction of the cloud of points and how spread out it is. A weak or noisy pattern would suggest that similar debt levels can still lead to very different financial outcomes.

Conclusion

What do these patterns suggest?

Higher education remains one of the most significant financial decisions students make. Across institutions, the relationship between tuition, borrowing, and earnings is not always straightforward. While certain degrees and institutions show stronger outcomes, others leave students with higher debt and less predictable financial returns. These patterns highlight the importance of understanding the economic trade-offs behind educational choices.
